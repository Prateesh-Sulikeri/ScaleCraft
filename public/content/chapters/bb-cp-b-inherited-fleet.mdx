Most systems you work on, you did not build. You join a team, someone shows
you a diagram, and the diagram looks fine: every box is connected, nothing is
red, and the site is up. The faults that matter in a system like that are not
broken wiring. They are the places where the drawing quietly disagrees with
what the system is supposed to do.

Group B taught four properties a fleet has to keep, one per chapter, and
breaking any of them leaves every box in place. This checkpoint hands you a
system with four faults in it: three of those properties broken, plus the most
common fault Group A taught.

There is no lesson above this canvas naming the fault, and no rule that will
find most of them. You get the requirements, the system, and a count. Auditing
a design against its requirements one line at a time is the skill, and it is
the same skill an interviewer is testing when they point at your diagram and
ask "is this ready for Monday?"

## Connections

Group B added no new boxes. Every chapter in it was about a property the boxes
you already had must keep:

| Chapter | The property it taught |
|---|---|
| 3.6 Stateless Services | Copies behind a load balancer are only interchangeable if none of them keeps anything the next request needs. |
| 3.7 Sessions & State Management | Whatever a request does need from earlier - a login, a cart - lives in a store every copy can reach. |
| 3.8 Horizontal Scaling | Capacity is planned for peak with one copy lost, not for peak. |
| 3.9 Service Discovery | A cached answer about where something lives must not outlive the window in which that answer changes. |

And one from Group A, because it is the most common way an inherited system goes
wrong: 3.1's firewall set to `allow-all` is drawn, connected and doing nothing.
Somebody always meant to change it back.

Two of these are numbers, and the brief gives you what you need for both. For
capacity: what one instance carries, what peak needs, and what happens when one
instance is gone. For the cached address: how long it is reused, against how long
the change takes.

## Your turn

**The system.** Last week you joined the team that runs a clinic
appointment-booking service. The diagram on the canvas is the system as it runs
today. It works, mostly, and four things in it are wrong.

What it is supposed to do:

- Patients reach it at one domain name, and the perimeter refuses anything it
  has not been told to allow.
- A patient who logs in stays logged in on their next request, whichever
  machine serves it.
- Peak is 450 requests a second, Monday at 08:00. One application instance
  handles 150, measured. Losing any one instance at peak must not drop
  requests.
- On Tuesday the whole stack moves to new infrastructure, with a 30-second
  cutover window. No patient may still be sent to the old address once the
  window has closed.

**How it is judged**: fix all four faults without rebuilding what already works.
Validate will point at one of them. The other three are legal drawings that
contradict a line of the brief, so no rule can see them; Submit checks each one
against the brief. If Submit says a component is missing while it is on the
canvas, it is reading a setting on that component.
