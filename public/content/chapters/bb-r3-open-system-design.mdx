Every exercise so far had one right shape, or two, and Submit checked your graph
against it. That is how a course teaches a component. It is not how a design is
judged in an interview or in a review, where many designs are acceptable and the
question is whether yours contains a mistake you should have known better than to
make.

This checkpoint switches to that posture. The brief is deliberately incomplete.
Many designs pass. Submit fails a design only for a mistake this course has
already taught - and when it does, the explanation is the one you read when you
learned it. It is the same posture every Real World Extraction project uses from
here on, met first with a palette you already know.

## Connections

What changes is not the material but what counts as wrong:

| | R1 and R2 | R3 |
|---|---|---|
| The brief | Complete: every requirement stated | Incomplete: you decide what matters and say so |
| What passes | A design containing the expected shape | Any design with no taught anti-pattern in it |
| What fails | A missing or misplaced component | A mistake the course named: a split brain, a queue whose failures vanish, a load balancer in front of one instance, a perimeter that filters nothing, a client wired to a database, a request path that loops, a connection the components themselves refuse |
| A warning | A note you could ignore | Taught anti-patterns fail; a stray unconnected card stays a note |

Two habits matter more than any component here. **Write your assumptions down** -
in a comment on the canvas, the way you would say them aloud to an interviewer -
because an incomplete brief means some of your decisions are guesses, and a
stated guess is a design decision while an unstated one is a gap. And **build
the smallest design that answers what you assumed**, then grow it only where a
requirement pushes: a palette of 27 does not mean a design of 27.

## Your turn

**The product.** A ticketing service for concerts and sports. Venues publish
events with seat maps; fans browse, search and buy tickets. Most of the year it
is quiet. When a major tour goes on sale, two million fans arrive in the first
ten minutes for 60,000 seats.

What you know:

- A seat can never be sold to two people.
- Fans receive their tickets by email.
- Venues need sales reports.
- When a big sale opens, the system must stay up for the fans who are buying,
  even if it cannot serve everyone who arrived.

That is all. How many venues, how fresh search must be, how long a seat may be
held at checkout, what happens to a fan who does not get in - you decide, and the
first thing on your canvas should be a comment saying what you decided.

**How it is judged**: Submit passes any design that takes requests in through
networking, does work in compute, and keeps or hands off data somewhere - and
contains none of the anti-patterns in the table above. Validate shows every
warning with its full explanation before you submit. The Debrief shows one
reference design afterwards; it is one answer, not the answer.
