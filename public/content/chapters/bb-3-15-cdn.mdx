3.14 moved the answer one hop from the code that needs it. The code is in
Virginia. A third of your users are not.

That product page returns in 40 ms near your servers and takes most of a second
in Sydney, on the same servers, at the same hit ratio. A Sydney-to-Virginia
round trip runs about 200 ms, and a fresh connection spends two of them on the
TCP and TLS handshakes before your first byte moves. The work took 40 ms;
distance took the rest. Most of what crossed it did not need to - the bulk of a
page's bytes are images, stylesheets and script bundles that are identical for
every visitor and change only when you deploy.

> [!NOTE]
> Think first: same page, same servers, same 95% hit ratio - 40 ms in Virginia,
> 600 ms in Sydney. Every lever so far (more instances, a replica, a cache) is a
> way of doing work faster or doing it less often. Which of them removes the
> other 560 ms? Commit to an answer before reading on.

## The same bet, about place

3.14's cache bet that an answer would be wanted again before it stopped being
true. A CDN bets it will be wanted by someone else near this user. It is a
network of edge nodes - servers in a few hundred cities, each running a cache in
front of your origin - and which one a user talks to is decided before their
request is sent. Some CDNs steer with anycast instead, announcing one address
from every location and letting the network deliver to the nearest; DNS is the
mechanism this system already has, and the one an interviewer will ask about.

## The two things the edge decides

- **`cacheTtlSeconds`** (default 3600) - how long an edge node may serve its copy
  before checking with the origin again. The same staleness contract 3.14 wrote
  as a number, one layer out and usually far longer: what sits here changes on
  deploy, not on write.
- **`cacheDynamicContent`** (default off) - whether responses computed per
  request may be cached at all. Off is the right default, for the reason in the
  next section.

## What belongs at the edge

Two properties, both required: the response is **identical for many users**, and
it **tolerates being up to its TTL out of date**. One without the other is not a
candidate.

| Response | At the edge | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `app.9f3c1.js`, a fingerprinted bundle | Yes | Same bytes for everyone, and the URL changes whenever the content does, so the copy can never be stale. |
| Product images, video segments | Yes | Identical per viewer, and the largest thing you ship. This is where the money is. |
| A listing page, same for every signed-out visitor | Yes, short TTL | Identical per visitor; the TTL is how far behind you are willing to be. |
| A signed-in user's account page | No | Identical for nobody. One copy served twice is one user reading another's data. |
| `POST /checkout` | No | Not a read. There is nothing to copy, and it has to change state at the origin. |

Pull is the default provisioning model: the edge fetches on its first miss, so
one user per location pays for it. Push uploads ahead of time - nobody pays a
first miss, but every location stores every object whether anyone there asks or
not. Pull for a long-tail catalog, push for a small set you know everyone is
about to want.

## Trade-offs

| Decision | What it buys | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Long edge TTL (hours to days) | Highest hit ratio, least origin traffic | A wrong file stays wrong at the edge for that long unless you purge it |
| Short edge TTL (seconds to minutes) | Publishing without a purge step | More origin traffic, and every miss crosses the ocean |
| Fingerprinted filenames plus a long TTL | Both: instant publish and a year-long TTL | The build has to fingerprint every asset and rewrite every reference to it |
| Caching HTML for signed-out visitors | Takes the origin off the path for most page views | You have to be certain no per-user data can ever reach that response |

## What breaks

- **The wrong person's page.** A response carrying one user's data is cached at
  the edge and handed to the next visitor. The cause is a cache key that ignores
  what the response actually varied on. This is the only failure here with
  consequences past latency.
- **The file you can't take back.** You shipped a broken bundle at a 24-hour
  TTL. A purge crosses every location, takes minutes rather than instants, and
  does nothing about the copies already sitting in browsers.
- **The origin herd after a purge.** Invalidate everything at once and every
  location misses at once: 3.14's stampede at a wider radius, with fixes of the
  same shape - staggered expiry, or a shield tier that collapses many edge misses
  into one origin fetch.
- **A dependency you don't operate.** A bad edge configuration takes your site
  down while your origin sits healthy, and the rollback is on someone else's
  control plane.

## What changes at scale

At 10x nothing structural changes: the edge absorbs it, and what you tune is
what's cacheable, not capacity. At 100x the catalog matters more than the
traffic - a long tail leaves most objects cold in most locations, so
per-location hit ratio falls and origin fetches grow with catalog size rather
than with users, which is what makes a shield tier worth its own hop. At 1000x
the cost centre is bytes, and the move is to stop treating the CDN as a cache in
front of one origin: the largest video services ship cache appliances into ISP
networks so the last hop happens inside the user's own provider.

## In production

Wikipedia serves most of its anonymous page views out of caching data centres
placed around the world rather than from the servers that render the pages, and
signed-in readers bypass those caches by design. That split is this chapter's
rule in operational form: the anonymous view of an article is identical for
millions of people and tolerates being slightly behind, and the signed-in view of
the same article is neither.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting everything behind it and turning on dynamic caching.** The rule is
  two properties, not one, and "it's a GET" is not the second.
- **Using TTL as the publishing mechanism.** Short TTLs pay origin traffic on
  every asset, forever, to solve a problem fingerprinted filenames solve
  permanently and for free.
- **Believing a purge reaches users.** It reaches edge nodes. Browsers keep
  honoring the `Cache-Control` they were already given.
- **Judging it by hit ratio alone.** A 99% hit ratio with your worst region at
  800 ms means the misses are landing on exactly the people the CDN was bought
  for.

## In an interview

High relevance for anything media-heavy or global. It lands at step 4 (a system
with users on more than one continent has an edge tier in its first
architecture) and step 7 (the trade-off you are expected to name is which
responses you are willing to serve from a copy).

What that sounds like at a senior level: *"Most of the bytes here are identical
for every user, so I'd put a CDN in front, DNS-steered so the routing decision
happens at resolution. Fingerprinted filenames with a long TTL, so publishing
never depends on a purge. Signed-out listing pages can take a short TTL, and
anything per-user stays off the edge entirely - the failure I care about there
isn't staleness, it's serving one user's page to another."*

## Connections

This is 3.14's bet with the axis rotated: a cache removes work, a CDN removes
distance, and the two stay independent - the cache is still what keeps the origin
from recomputing whatever the edge misses on. It also closes 3.2's loop, which
ended on changing what a name resolves to being how you route traffic; steering
a user to a nearby edge is that mechanism's most common job, and it is why the
CDN is chosen at resolution rather than by anything downstream of it. And it is
3.3's reverse proxy in a few hundred places at once, operated by someone else.

cannot answer it well at any distance.

## Recap

- A cache bets an answer will be wanted again; a CDN bets it will be wanted
  nearby. Distance is not work, so none of the earlier levers touch it.
- A response belongs at the edge only if it is identical for many users and
  tolerates being up to its TTL out of date. Both, or neither.
- Fingerprinted filenames plus a long TTL beat a short TTL: a URL that changes
  with its content can never serve a stale copy.
- Pull makes one user per location pay a first miss; push makes every location
  store everything.
- The failure that matters is a per-user response cached at the edge, not a stale
  one.
