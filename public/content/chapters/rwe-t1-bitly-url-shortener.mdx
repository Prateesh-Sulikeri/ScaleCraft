R1 handed you a product and an empty canvas, and every requirement in it named
its own answer once you read it carefully. A brief is not usually that tidy.

Here is one that is not. A link shortener: paste a long URL, get back
`scr.at/7Qb4mK9`, and anyone who opens that lands on the original. Two
endpoints. One table's worth of data. It is the smallest real system in this
curriculum and it is the one interviewers open with, because almost every
decision in it is a trade-off with no default.

> [!NOTE]
> Think first: 40 million links are created a month, and each one is opened
> about a thousand times before it goes quiet. Which half of this system do you
> size, and what does that make the other half? Commit to an answer before
> reading on.

## The numbers pick the shape

| Measured | Figure | What it forces |
|---|---|---|
| New links | 40 M/month, ~15/second | The write path can be one boring machine |
| Redirects | ~15,000/second at peak | 1,000:1. Every design decision is a read decision |
| Retention | 5 years, ~2.4 billion rows at ~500 bytes | ~1.2 TB. One machine still holds it |
| Concentration | ~70% of a day's redirects land on under 10,000 links | The hot set is a few megabytes |

Read the last two rows together, because they are the whole design. The data is
too big to be interesting and the working set is small enough to be free. You
are not building a storage system. You are building a lookup that has to be
cheap 15,000 times a second, sitting on top of a store that is barely working.

The access pattern has exactly one shape: given a seven-character key, return
one URL. No joins, no ranges, no filters, no sorting. 3.11 gave you the question
to ask of any store - what does the query actually look like - and this is the
cleanest answer that question has ever had.

Note: the write path never touches the Cache. A newly created link is cold by
definition, and populating it on write would fill memory with links nobody has
clicked yet.

## Making a short key

Seven base62 characters is 3.5 trillion addresses for 2.4 billion links. Two
ways to pick one, and they fail differently.

**Hash the URL.** Take a digest of the long URL, keep the first seven base62
characters. The same URL always produces the same key, so duplicates collapse
for free. The catch is the birthday problem: at 2.4 billion keys in a 3.5
trillion space you expect on the order of 800,000 collisions, not a handful. A
key space being 1,400 times larger than the thing you are storing does not make
collisions rare, it makes them routine. So every write reads the store first to
check whether that key is taken, and re-hashes with a salt if it is. At 15
writes a second, an extra read is free.

**Count.** Keep a monotonic integer, base62-encode it. Collisions are
impossible by construction and no read is needed. Two costs. The counter is
shared state that every writer touches, which is the coordination point 3.6
spent a chapter teaching you to avoid - so each App Server claims a block of
10,000 ids at a time and the counter is touched once per block instead of once
per write. And sequential keys are enumerable: anyone can walk `aaaaaab`,
`aaaaaac` and read every link in your system, which for a product whose links
are often semi-private is a real leak, not a theoretical one.

> **Engineering lens.** Neither option is wrong. The hash pays per write to buy
> unguessable keys; the counter pays in coordination and privacy to buy
> certainty. What decides it is whether your links are meant to be secret,
> which is a product question the architecture cannot answer for you.

## 301 or 302

The redirect's HTTP status code is the highest-leverage decision in this system
and it is one character wide.

A **301** means permanent. The browser caches it, intermediaries cache it, and
after the first click that link's traffic never reaches you again. Your read
path gets cheaper than any cache you could build.

A **302** means temporary. Every single click comes back to you.

| | 301 Permanent | 302 Found |
|---|---|---|
| Buys | Redirect traffic disappears at the client | Every click is yours to count |
| Costs | No click counts, and the link can never be repointed | You carry all 15,000/second yourself |

Which is why Bitly serves 302s. Click analytics is the product being sold, and a
link that can be repointed after it has been printed on something is the feature
enterprise customers pay for. Both require the click to arrive. A shortener
whose links are disposable and uncounted would be right to choose 301 and
delete most of this architecture.

## Trade-offs

| Decision | What it buys | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| Key-value store | The query is literally `get(key)`, and it cannot get slower as the table grows | Nothing relational later: click aggregation becomes a separate job |
| SQL primary plus a replica | One engine you already operate, and reporting queries stay possible | Replication lag: a link created a second ago can 404 if its read lands on a replica |
| Longer TTL on hot keys | Higher hit ratio, fewer reads reaching the store | A repointed link keeps sending people to the old destination until it expires |
| Edge-caching the redirect | The response never crosses an ocean | Only works for 301s, so it costs you the analytics again |

The last row is the one people miss. A CDN cannot help a 302, because a 302 is
by definition not cacheable at the edge for long. The read path and the business
model are the same decision.

## What breaks

- **A link goes viral and its key expires.** Every in-flight request misses at
  the same instant and the store takes the full rate for one key. 3.14's
  stampede, arriving on the one system where a single key can carry a
  meaningful share of all traffic.
- **The 404 on a link that exists.** A user shortens a URL and clicks it
  immediately; the read lands on a replica that has not received the row yet.
  The link is real, the store says no, and the user concludes the product is
  broken. Serve reads for recently created links from the primary, or write the
  key to the fast tier as part of the create.
- **The cold cache after a restart.** The store has been sized for post-cache
  traffic for eighteen months. The fast tier restarts empty and 15,000 requests
  a second arrive at a machine that has not seen 200 in a year.
- **The hottest key ignores your partitioning.** 3.13 splits load by key, and
  the single most-clicked link in the system is one key. Partitioning cannot
  help it. Only the memory in front of it can.

## What changes at scale

At **10x**, nothing structural moves. Hit ratio is the only number worth
tuning, and the store is still mostly idle.

At **100x** the hot set outgrows one machine's memory, and that machine is now
a single point of failure in front of the entire read path - which is the exact
condition 3.14 named for replacing it with a partitioned, replicated tier.

At **1000x** the constraint stops being throughput and becomes geography. A 302
served from one region is 200 ms of dead time for half the planet, and the fix
is a copy of the hot set near the user. That reopens the 301 question with real
money attached.

## In production

Bitly's redirects are 302s, which is a deliberate purchase: serving every click
yourself is the cost of selling click analytics and of letting a customer
repoint a link after it has shipped. Twitter's `t.co` makes the opposite bet
about scope - every link in every tweet is wrapped, so the shortener is on the
critical path of the timeline itself, and it is sized so that the redirect is
never the slow part of opening a link. Same component, two different reasons
for it to exist.

## Common mistakes

- **Designing the write path first.** It runs at 15 requests a second. Time
  spent sharding it is time not spent on the path carrying a thousand times more
  traffic.
- **Choosing 301 for the latency win** without pricing what it deletes. It is
  the fastest possible answer and it silently ends the analytics product.
- **Generating keys randomly with no collision check.** Random and
  collision-free are not the same property, and the collision shows up as one
  user's link quietly pointing at another user's destination.
- **Calling the store "fast enough" and skipping the fast tier.** It is fast
  enough. It is also answering 1.3 billion lookups a day that nobody needed it
  to answer.

## In an interview

High relevance, and it is graded on step 3 more than step 4: the interviewer is
watching whether you estimate the read/write ratio before you draw anything, and
a candidate who opens with boxes has already lost the point. The deep dive
(step 5) is almost always key generation, and the trade-off they wait for
(step 7) is the redirect code.

What that sounds like at a senior level: *"Reads beat writes about a thousand to
one, so I'm sizing the redirect path and leaving the create path deliberately
boring. The lookup is a single key fetch, so a key-value store with a shared
cache tier in front of it, hit ratio being the number that matters rather than
store latency. For keys I'd hash and check for a collision rather than counting,
because sequential keys are enumerable and these links are often semi-private. And
I'd serve 302s, knowing that means carrying every click - if we weren't selling
analytics I'd argue for 301 and most of this tier would disappear."*

## Connections

This is 3.11's question with the cleanest possible answer: one key in, one value
out, which is what a key-value store is for, and which is also why choosing a
relational primary here is defensible rather than wrong - you would be buying
reporting flexibility with replication lag. 3.14 supplies the entire read path,
and the hit ratio is not a tuning detail here but the design itself. 3.4, 3.6
and 3.8 supply the tier that carries it, unchanged from the shape you have drawn
since Group B. What is new is that none of them were named for you.

## Recap

- 1,000:1 reads to writes means every real decision is a read decision. The
  create path is allowed to be dull.
- The access pattern is `get(key)`. That is the whole argument for a key-value
  store, and the whole cost of not using one.
- Hash keys need a collision check; counter keys need coordination and leak an
  enumerable namespace. Product secrecy decides it, not performance.
- 301 deletes your traffic and your analytics together. 302 keeps both and makes
  you pay for them.
- The hot set is megabytes. Whatever holds it is the most load-bearing thing on
  the canvas.

## Your turn

**The product.** A link shortener, four months old, currently one application
server talking to one relational database. It works. The numbers above are next
quarter's, not today's, and the rebuild is being scoped now.

What it has to do:

- A seven-character key resolves to its destination in single-digit
  milliseconds, 15,000 times a second at peak, without that work reaching the
  store that owns the links on most requests.
- Creating a link runs about 15 times a second and may stay simple. It must not
  be made complicated to match the other path.
- 2.4 billion links over five years live in something whose query is a single
  key lookup.
- Losing the machine that runs application code drops no requests.
- One public hostname, reached from a browser, resolving to whatever fronts the
  request tier.
- A link opened one second after it was created must not 404.

**The exercise.** Get one redirect served correctly and cheaply: the path from
the browser to whatever owns the link, with repeat reads absorbed before they
reach it. Then commit to a store and own its consequences. More than one
architecture passes, and the two that do disagree about something real. Validate
runs every rule in the registry against your graph, not a curated subset, so
anti-patterns from any chapter will speak up.

**Stretch, optional and not graded.** Nothing here throttles the create
endpoint, and nothing here answers what a second continent would change. Both
are the next two Tier 1 projects.

**What you are not told**: which store, how many of anything, or where the
repeat reads should be absorbed. The brief above is symptoms and rates.
