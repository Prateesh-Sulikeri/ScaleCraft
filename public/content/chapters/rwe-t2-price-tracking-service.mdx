A price tracker: users paste a product link, set a target price, and get an alert
when the price drops below it. Version one ran a cron job every hour that fetched
every tracked product page from the retailer. At 300,000 products that took 20
minutes. At 2 million it took three hours, the runs overlapped, and the
retailer's bot defense blocked the service's addresses for 48 hours.

The block landed during a two-day sale. 140,000 users had alerts set on products
that dropped below their targets and came back up, and none of them heard about
it.

> [!NOTE]
> Think first: you are a guest on someone else's site, and it decides how often you
> may ask. With far more products than requests you are allowed, which products
> should be checked, how often, and how do you notice a drop without re-reading
> every user's alert? Commit to an answer before reading on.

## The numbers

Interview steps 1 to 3. One retailer carries most of the catalog; the rest are
smaller sites with their own limits.

| Measured | Figure | What it forces |
|---|---|---|
| Tracked products | 20 million distinct pages | The unit of work is a fetch, and fetches are rationed |
| Watches | 50 million (user, product, target price) | Matching a price change to watchers is its own problem |
| Fetch budget | ~200 requests/s across all retailers, 120/s for the largest | 17 million fetches a day. Checking every product hourly would need 5,600/s, 28x the budget |
| Popularity | 1% of products carry 60% of watches | Uniform checking wastes the budget on pages nobody is waiting on |
| Price changes | Popular products: about daily. The tail: monthly | Most fetches find nothing new |
| Freshness | A drop on a popular product should alert within an hour | Sales are short; an alert after the price came back is worthless |

The website half (users, watches, the alert settings page) is a few hundred
requests a second and entirely familiar. The constraint that shapes everything is
on the other side: a budget you do not control, against a catalog 28 times too big
for it.

## Spending a fetch budget

Divided evenly, 200 fetches a second checks each product once every 28 hours. The
cadence has to follow value instead:

- **Score each product.** Watchers, how often its price has moved, and how long
  since it was checked. The planner checks products whose next check time has
  come, highest score first.
- **Adapt the interval.** A check that finds a change halves that product's
  interval (to a floor of 30 minutes); a check that finds nothing doubles it (to a
  ceiling of 7 days). The budget drifts toward products that are actually moving.
- **Govern the rate per retailer.** The planner may make 12,000 products due in one
  minute. They go on a queue, and the fetch workers for each retailer run at a
  fixed concurrency sized to its budget. The queue absorbs the burst; the
  consumers decide the rate. This is 3.24's token bucket applied to yourself.
- **Listen when told to slow down.** A 429 or 503 from a retailer backs off the
  whole retailer, not just that request, and honors `Retry-After`. Repeated errors
  open a circuit (3.23) for that retailer while the others continue.

| Tier | Products | Interval | Fetches a day |
|---|---|---|---|
| Popular (1%) | 200,000 | 1 hour | 4.8 million |
| Middle | 2 million | 12 hours | 4 million |
| Tail | 17.8 million | 7 days | 2.5 million |
| **Total** | 20 million | | **11.3 million, ~130/s** |

That leaves a third of the budget for adaptive rechecks and new products. Where a
retailer publishes a product feed or an affiliate API, one call covers thousands of
products and the rationing problem shrinks.

> **Engineering lens.** When a resource you depend on is rationed, the design
> question changes from "how fast can we go" to "what is each unit of the ration
> worth". The planner is a prioritization function, not a loop.

## Detecting change and alerting

A fetch is only useful if it notices something real, and only then does the
expensive work begin. Four steps, each with a trap:

- **Normalize.** The same page shows a different price to a different region, a
  logged-in user, or an A/B bucket, and a third-party seller's offer can replace the
  main one. The fetcher extracts price, currency, availability and seller under one
  fixed context, or it detects changes that are artifacts of how it looked.
- **Compare to the last observation.** The fetcher reads the last known price and
  publishes an event only when it differs. 95% of fetches end here, quietly.
- **Match watchers by index.** A drop to $199 on product 88213 matches the watches
  on 88213 whose target is $199 or more. Watches are indexed by (product, target),
  so that is a range read over one product's watches, not a scan of 50 million
  rows.
- **Alert once per crossing.** A price that flaps between $99 and $101 around a
  $100 target would alert every time it dips. Each watch alerts on a crossing and
  re-arms only after the price rises back above target by a margin, or after a
  cooldown.

The change event has more than one consumer: alerts, the price-history chart, and
the "biggest drops today" page. Each is derived data built from the same stream
(3.16's idea), and each should be able to fall behind or be rebuilt without holding
up the others.

> **Production lens.** Most of a tracker's bugs are false changes, not missed ones:
> a currency switch, a regional price, a seller swap. Every one becomes an alert a
> user did not ask for.

## Trade-offs

| Decision | One way | The other way |
|---|---|---|
| How change events reach their consumers | An event bus fanning out to each subscriber: simple, and a subscriber that was down misses what it could not receive | A partitioned log with a consumer group per use: each consumer replays from its own offset, so the alert matcher can be fixed and re-run over yesterday's changes, at the cost of a log to operate |
| Cadence | Fixed tiers by popularity: predictable load | Adaptive per product: the budget follows actual movement, but a product that changes after months of stability is caught late |
| Where the last price lives | Read from the history store per fetch: one source of truth | Kept in a fast store keyed by product: cheaper per fetch, one more thing to keep consistent |
| Fetching | Scraping pages: works for any retailer, fragile to layout changes | A retailer's feed or API: cheap and stable, but only where one is offered |
| Alert timing | Immediately on each crossing | Batched into a digest: fewer messages, and a short sale may end before the digest goes out |

## What breaks

- **Overlapping runs.** The cold open: a fetch-everything job that takes longer than
  its interval runs twice at once and doubles the request rate.
- **Layout changes.** The retailer ships a new page template and every parse fails.
  Without a dead-letter path, failing products retry forever and burn the budget on
  pages that cannot be read; with one, they are set aside and a parser fix replays
  them.
- **False drops.** A page served in another currency reads as a 70% drop and
  alerts 300,000 people.
- **The popular-product burst.** A real drop on a product with 300,000 watchers
  produces 300,000 alerts in a second. They go to a notification pipeline at a
  rate it can take, not straight to the providers.

## What changes at scale

At **10x** products (200 million), the budget is the system: you cannot buy more of
someone else's tolerance. Feeds, partnerships and crowd-sourced observations (users'
browser extensions reporting the prices they see) replace fetching for most of the
catalog.

At **100x** watches, matching moves from the database index to an in-memory
structure per hot product, rebuilt from the watches table, because the top products'
watcher lists are read thousands of times a minute.

At **1000x** events, price history stops being rows: it is downsampled into hourly
and daily points after a week, the same retention trade every time-series store
makes.

## In production

**Camelcamelcamel**, the long-running Amazon price tracker, alerts on target prices
and charts history, and relies heavily on Amazon's affiliate product data rather
than scraping every page. The budget problem is solved by not fetching where the
retailer will hand you the data.

**Googlebot** sets its crawl rate per host from how the host responds, and slows
down when it starts seeing 429s, 500s or 503s, because a crawler that overloads a
site gets blocked and then has no data at all. Politeness is not courtesy; it is how
the crawler keeps working.

A small tracker checks its few thousand products every few hours from one machine
with a per-host delay, and needs none of the scoring until the catalog outgrows the
budget.

## Common mistakes

- **Checking everything on a fixed timer.** The budget runs out at a catalog size you
  will reach.
- **Enforcing politeness in the scheduler instead of at the fetchers.** The planner's
  bursts reach the retailer unless something with a fixed rate sits in between.
- **Scanning watches on every change.** Index by product and target.
- **Alerting on every dip.** A flapping price becomes spam without re-arm logic.

## In an interview

"Design a price tracker" sounds like CRUD plus a cron job, and step 1 is where that
reading is corrected: whose limits apply, how many products, how fresh. Step 3 is
the decisive moment - products times frequency against the budget - and the answer
"28 times over" reshapes the design. The deep dive (step 5) is cadence and change
detection; the follow-up is "a popular product just dropped, what happens?"

What that sounds like at a senior level: *"Fetches are rationed by the retailer, so
the planner is a priority function: watchers, volatility and staleness, with
intervals that halve on change and double on no change. Due products go on a queue,
and the fetchers for each retailer run at a concurrency matched to its budget and
back off the whole host on 429s. A fetch publishes an event only when the normalized
price changes; the alert matcher reads watches indexed by product and target, alerts
once per crossing, and hands alerts to the notification pipeline. Price history is
another consumer of the same events."*

## Connections

3.19's Cron Job is still the clock, but it now starts a planner rather than the
work itself, which is the same split 3.17 made between accepting work and doing it.
3.17's queue and workers become a rate governor: the queue absorbs whatever the
planner produces, the fixed worker pool sets the pace. 3.18's fan-out carries one
change to several consumers, and 3.16's derived data is what those consumers build.
3.24 and 3.23 show up turned around: you obey someone else's limit and open a
circuit when they push back.

## Recap

- A fetch budget you do not control, against a catalog 28 times too big: the planner
  decides what each fetch is worth.
- Score by watchers, volatility and staleness; halve the interval on change, double
  it on silence.
- A queue plus fixed-concurrency fetchers turns any burst into the retailer's
  allowed rate.
- Publish only real changes; match watchers by an index on product and target.
- Alert once per crossing, or a flapping price becomes spam.

## Your turn

**The product.** The tracker from the opening: browsers reach one web server that
stores watches in a relational database. An hourly cron job starts a fetcher that
reads every product page as fast as it can and writes prices to the same database.

What it has to do:

- Requests to retailers stay within their budget, however many products come due in
  the same minute, and a product whose page keeps failing to parse is set aside
  rather than retried forever.
- A price change reaches the users whose target it crosses, and also feeds price
  history, without either one waiting on the other.
- Losing one machine that serves the website drops no requests.
- One public hostname resolves to whatever fronts the website.

**The exercise.** Replace fetch-everything with checking that respects the budget,
and connect observed changes to the people waiting on them. How change events reach
their consumers is yours to decide; more than one answer passes. Validate runs every
rule in the registry; a queue with nowhere for failed work to go is an error on this
project.

**Stretch, optional and not graded.** The largest retailer cuts your budget from
120 to 40 requests a second. Which products lose freshness first under your planner,
and what would you ask the retailer for instead?

**What you are not told**: what plans the checks, how change events are carried, or
how many consumers they have.
