Checkpoint D asked for a site that stays up: one request path, from a browser to
the data behind it, with every stop answering a requirement. Everything since
has been about what that path cannot do on its own - work that should happen later, bytes
that are not rows, data that spans machines, and machines that fail.

Checkpoints E and F each tested one of those groups inside a system that was
already standing. This one asks for all of it at once, on an empty canvas. Nothing new is
taught, and nothing in the brief needs anything you have not built. What is new
is the sorting: ten requirements, three kinds of path - the request someone waits
on, the work that happens after they leave, and the work nobody asked for - and
no lesson above the canvas saying which is which. It is the largest design in
Building Blocks, and the closest thing in it to what an interviewer means by
"design the backend for this".

## Connections

Checkpoint D covered Groups A-D. Three more groups since, each asking a
question Checkpoint D's design could not answer:

| Group | The question it answered |
|---|---|
| A-D (3.1-3.16, Checkpoint D) | How does a request reach an answer quickly, past the perimeter, on machines that can be replaced? |
| E, Asynchronous Systems (3.17-3.19) | Which work should not happen while the user waits, and what starts the work nobody asked for? |
| F, Storage (3.20-3.22) | Where do bytes that are not rows live, and what does "written" mean once data spans machines? |
| G, Reliability (3.23-3.26) | What happens when a call is slow, a caller misbehaves, nobody is watching, or the machine taking writes dies? |

Three things are easy to lose at this size. A requirement about *time* - "must
not slow the order", "every night" - is a requirement about which path the work
is on, not about speed. A requirement about *failure* - "no person involved",
"never two machines" - is answered by structure, not by hoping. And two different
stores for two different kinds of data is not duplication: the catalog and the
orders want different answers to 3.22's question, and the brief says which.

## Your turn

**The product.** An outdoor-gear retailer selling online. 800,000 products from
2,000 sellers, 300,000 shoppers on a normal day, ten times that on the two sale
days a year.

What it has to do:

- Shoppers arrive at one public domain name in a browser. A third of them are on
  another continent, and every visitor downloads the same storefront bundle
  before the first page renders.
- Nothing reaches application code that was not filtered at the perimeter first,
  and the default is closed. The certificate is terminated in one place. API
  callers are authenticated and held to a per-caller request limit in one place.
- Sale-day peak is far more than one machine can carry, and one machine dying
  mid-sale must not drop requests.
- Shoppers search the catalog in free text, and the cost of answering does not
  grow with its 800,000 products.
- The 5,000 most popular product pages are read hundreds of times for every
  change to them.
- The catalog - names, prices, stock - is owned by exactly one store.
- Sellers upload product photos: 40 TB and growing. They must not live in the
  store that holds the catalog rows.
- Placing an order answers in under a second. The confirmation email takes 2 to
  6 seconds through the email provider, which sometimes fails for an hour; it
  must not slow the order down, and no confirmation may be silently lost.
- Orders are the data that must not go wrong. If the machine accepting orders
  dies, orders are accepted again within seconds with no person involved; no
  order a shopper was told succeeded is ever lost; and two machines never accept
  orders at the same time.
- Every night at 01:00 a sales report aggregates the day's orders. It takes about
  25 minutes, starts on its own, runs once, and must not touch either the machine
  accepting orders or the pool serving shoppers.

**What you are not told**: which requirement each component answers, what order
they go in, how many of anything you need, or which requirements share a
component. Validate reports connections the components themselves refuse and the
taught warnings; it does not report a requirement with nothing answering it.
